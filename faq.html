Answers to the most common questions about farm loans, USDA programs, crop insurance, equipment financing, and agricultural lenders.

Farm Loans

What types of farm loans are available?
Farm loans fall into four main categories: operating loans (fund annual inputs — 1–7 year terms), farm ownership loans (finance land purchases — 20–40 year terms), equipment loans (machinery and technology — 3–10 year terms), and emergency loans (disaster recovery after presidential or USDA declarations). FSA, Farm Credit, and commercial lenders each offer variations of these with different rates, terms, and qualification requirements.
What credit score do I need for a farm loan?
Requirements vary by lender type. FSA doesn't use a minimum FICO score — they assess creditworthiness through their own criteria. Farm Credit typically requires 660–680+. Commercial agricultural banks generally require 660–700. Online lenders like National Funding and Fora Financial work with scores as low as 500–570. The best rates universally go to borrowers with 720+ scores and strong farm cash flow.
How much can I borrow for a farm loan?
Maximums depend on the lender and program. FSA Direct loans cap at $600,000 for both operating and ownership. FSA Guaranteed loans (commercial lenders with USDA backing) allow up to $2.24 million. Farm Credit has no set cap — associations fund loans exceeding $50 million. Commercial banks typically max at $1–5 million. Your actual capacity depends on cash flow, collateral, and credit quality.
What is the current interest rate on farm loans?
As of 2026: FSA Direct farm ownership loans run approximately 4.75% fixed (set quarterly by U.S. Treasury). Farm Credit rates start around 5.85% for qualified borrowers, effectively lower after 15–25% patronage dividends. Commercial bank farm real estate runs 6.5–7.5%, operating lines 7.5–9.0%. Online lenders like National Funding and Fora charge 15–35% for short-term working capital.
How long does it take to get a farm loan?
Speed varies dramatically by lender. National Funding: 24 hours. Fora Financial: 24–72 hours. Commercial banks: 1–3 weeks. Farm Credit: 2–4 weeks. FSA Direct: 30–60 days (sometimes longer). The more documentation required and the larger the loan, the longer the timeline. Incomplete applications are the #1 cause of delays at every lender type.
Can I get a farm loan with no down payment?
FSA Beginning Farmer loans require only 5% down (vs. 10% standard). Some Farm Credit associations offer 95% financing for highly qualified beginning farmers. Working capital lenders like National Funding and Fora Financial don't require down payments because their products are unsecured. For standard farm real estate, expect 10–25% down depending on lender, loan type, and credit quality.
What collateral do farm loans require?
Most farm loans are secured by the asset being financed: land for ownership loans, equipment for equipment loans, crops/livestock for operating loans. FSA and Farm Credit always require collateral. The exception is short-term working capital from online lenders (National Funding, Fora), which use general UCC liens rather than specific asset pledges — but at much higher rates.
Can I have multiple farm loans at once?
Yes — most farmers work with 2–3 lenders simultaneously. A common structure: FSA or Farm Credit for long-term land debt (lowest rates), a local bank for the annual operating line, and a dealer finance company for equipment. Lenders coordinate lien positions and expect this arrangement. Your primary real estate lender typically holds first position on the land.

USDA & FSA Programs

What is the FSA and what loans do they offer?
The Farm Service Agency (FSA) is the USDA division that provides direct and guaranteed farm loans. Programs include: Direct Farm Ownership Loans (up to $600K, ~4.75% fixed), Direct Operating Loans (up to $600K), Microloans (up to $50K, simplified), Emergency Loans (disaster recovery), and the Down Payment Program. FSA is the lender of last resort — designed for farmers who cannot get credit elsewhere at reasonable terms.
What is the difference between FSA Direct and Guaranteed loans?
Direct loans come from FSA itself at the lowest government-set rates. Guaranteed loans are made by commercial lenders (banks, Farm Credit) with USDA guaranteeing 90–95% against default — letting the lender offer better terms. Direct loans cap at $600,000; Guaranteed loans go to $2.24 million. Direct loans have more requirements but lower rates; Guaranteed loans are faster and larger.
How do I qualify for an FSA loan?
Requirements: U.S. citizen or permanent resident, demonstrate ability to repay, acceptable credit history (FSA's own criteria, not a FICO minimum), unable to obtain credit elsewhere at reasonable terms, sufficient farm management experience or completion of borrower training, and for ownership loans — operate the farm as your primary occupation.
What is the FSA Beginning Farmer program?
Beginning Farmer status (less than 10 years of farming experience) provides: priority loan processing, reduced down payments (5% vs. 10%), reserved allocation of approximately 50% of FSA's direct loan budget, eligibility for the Down Payment Program, and up to $50,000 in microloans with simplified paperwork. This is one of the most valuable federal agricultural programs.
How long does the FSA application process take?
Allow 30–60 days from completed application to loan closing. Steps: submission review (3–5 days), credit evaluation (5–10 days), property appraisal for real estate (10–20 days), loan committee review (5–10 days), and document preparation/signing. Microloans are faster — typically 2–3 weeks. The #1 cause of delays is incomplete documentation.
What happens if my FSA loan application is denied?
FSA must provide written explanation of denial and your appeal rights. Common reasons: insufficient cash flow (revise business plan), failure to prove credit elsewhere unavailable (get denial letters from 2+ commercial lenders), inadequate experience (complete borrower training through Extension). You can appeal the decision or reapply after addressing the deficiency.
What is a Marketing Assistance Loan (MAL)?
MALs are short-term USDA loans that let you pledge harvested crops as collateral rather than selling at low post-harvest prices. You store grain, take the loan at the county loan rate, and repay or forfeit the commodity later. If prices rise above the loan rate, you repay and keep the profit. MALs provide marketing flexibility without a cash flow crunch.
What is the Farm Storage Facility Loan (FSFL) program?
FSFL provides low-interest loans up to $500,000 for building or upgrading farm storage — grain bins, hay barns, cold storage, and drying equipment. Terms run 7–12 years at below-market rates. You must produce a qualifying commodity and meet basic credit requirements. The program helps farmers store commodities and sell at better prices.

Crop Insurance

What is crop insurance and how does it work?
Crop insurance is a federally subsidized risk management program protecting farmers against crop losses from natural disasters, weather, disease, or revenue shortfalls. The USDA Risk Management Agency (RMA) oversees the program. Private Approved Insurance Providers (AIPs) sell policies. The federal government subsidizes 50–67% of premiums. Coverage is based on your Actual Production History (APH) and market prices.
How much does crop insurance cost?
Premiums depend on coverage level, county, crop, and your yield history. At 70% coverage, federal subsidies cover about 59% of the total premium. For a typical 500-acre Midwest corn farm, net premiums run $15–$30 per acre after subsidies. Higher coverage levels (80–85%) cost more but provide a tighter safety net. Premiums are tax-deductible as a farm business expense.
What is Revenue Protection (RP) insurance?
RP covers both yield losses AND price declines — it pays when your actual revenue per acre falls below the guaranteed level. The guarantee uses the higher of base price (spring) or harvest price, providing enhanced price protection. About 80% of insured acres carry RP because it covers the most common loss scenario: prices dropping during the growing season.
When is the deadline to buy crop insurance?
Sales closing dates vary by crop and region. Spring-planted crops (corn, soybeans): March 15 in most states. Winter wheat: September 30. There are no extensions or exceptions. Contact your agent 2–3 weeks before your deadline. Coverage begins at planting and runs through harvest. You can adjust coverage levels annually before the deadline.
Do beginning farmers get crop insurance discounts?
Yes — beginning farmers receive an additional 10 percentage point premium subsidy for their first 5 years. At 70% coverage, your subsidy becomes 69% instead of 59%. Catastrophic (CAT) coverage has $0 premium (only $655 admin fee). These benefits significantly reduce the cost of risk management for new operations.
How does crop insurance affect my farm loan?
Most lenders require crop insurance — typically 70%+ coverage. Insurance protects both you and the lender: disaster indemnity helps repay operating loans. Higher coverage may unlock more operating credit. Many lenders take assignment of insurance proceeds. Some lenders require you to forward-contract a portion of insured production.
What is Actual Production History (APH)?
APH is your farm's proven yield history — the average of 4–10 years of actual yields for a specific crop in a county. Higher APH = higher insurance guarantee = more protection (and higher premiums). Build APH by filing annual production reports with your agent. New farmers without history receive a transitional yield (typically 60–100% of county average).
What is Whole-Farm Revenue Protection (WFRP)?
WFRP covers your entire farming operation's revenue under one policy — ideal for diversified operations with multiple crops, livestock, or specialty products. It covers up to $17 million in revenue and provides up to 85% coverage. WFRP uses Schedule F tax returns as the basis for coverage, making it simpler for diversified farms than carrying multiple individual policies.

Farm Lenders

What is the Farm Credit System?
Farm Credit is a nationwide network of borrower-owned cooperative lending institutions chartered by Congress in 1916. Borrowers become member-owners and receive annual patronage dividends (15–30% of interest paid back). Farm Credit lends exclusively to agriculture and rural America. The system includes 67 associations with over $380 billion in assets. It is not a government agency — it is a cooperative.
How do Farm Credit patronage dividends work?
Each year, your Farm Credit association distributes net earnings proportional to interest paid. If you paid $10,000 in interest and the patronage rate is 20%, you receive $2,000 back — effectively reducing your rate by about 1 percentage point. Rates vary by association and year (typically 15–30%). Dividends are taxable income in the year received.
How do online lenders compare to FSA and Farm Credit?
Online lenders offer speed (24-hour funding) and accessibility (500+ credit scores) at higher cost (15–35% APR). FSA offers lowest rates (4.75%) but takes 30–60 days. Farm Credit offers competitive rates plus patronage dividends but requires 680+ credit. Choose based on your priority: speed vs. cost vs. accessibility.
What should I ask a farm lender before applying?
Essential questions: What is the total effective rate including fees? Prepayment penalties? Collateral requirements? Seasonal payment options? Workout process if I miss payments? Timeline from application to funding? Do I need crop insurance? What loan-to-value will you accept? Do you offer patronage dividends? What documentation do you need?
Can I switch or refinance between farm lenders?
Yes — you can refinance from one lender to another at any time. Common moves: bank to Farm Credit (for patronage), online lender to FSA (for rate savings), or consolidating multiple loans. Most farm loans have no prepayment penalty (FSA never does). The new lender pays off your existing balance and establishes new terms.
What happens if I can't repay my farm loan?
Federal law requires FSA to offer servicing options before foreclosure: reamortization, rescheduling, deferral, and write-down. Farm Credit must offer similar restructuring. Commercial lenders have more discretion but prefer workout plans. Agricultural mediation (free in most states) helps negotiate. Contact your lender immediately if you anticipate difficulty — early communication produces better outcomes.

Equipment Financing

Should I buy or lease farm equipment?
Buy when: keeping 7+ years, want equity, need Section 179 deductions, no usage restrictions. Lease when: upgrading frequently, preserving cash, equipment becomes obsolete quickly, prefer fixed budgeting. Run both scenarios through our lease vs. buy calculator — the answer depends on your tax situation, cash flow, and equipment lifecycle.
What is Section 179 for farm equipment?
Section 179 lets you deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment in the year of purchase — up to $1.16 million for 2026. This can dramatically reduce your tax liability in high-income years. Most farm machinery, vehicles, and technology qualify. The equipment must be placed in service during the tax year. Leased equipment does not qualify.
How much down payment do equipment loans require?
Typically 10–20% for new equipment, 15–25% for used. AgDirect and Farm Credit sometimes offer 100% financing for qualified borrowers with strong equity. Online lenders structure differently — no traditional down payment but higher rates. Used equipment over 10 years old may require 25–35% down due to depreciation risk.
What equipment qualifies for agricultural financing?
Virtually all production equipment: tractors, combines, sprayers, planters, tillage, grain bins, dryers, livestock equipment, irrigation systems, precision ag technology (GPS, variable rate), ATVs/UTVs, and farm trucks. The key requirement is agricultural use. Some lenders also finance rural lifestyle equipment (mowers, small tractors) under ag programs.
How long are farm equipment loan terms?
Terms typically match useful life: tractors 5–7 years, combines 5–7 years, irrigation 7–12 years, grain storage 10–15 years, precision ag 3–5 years. Shorter terms = higher payments but less total interest. AgDirect and Farm Credit offer flexible seasonal payment structures that align with your harvest income.

Beginning Farmers

What qualifies me as a beginning farmer?
USDA defines beginning farmers as operators with less than 10 years of experience. You must not own a farm larger than 30% of county average (for FSA). Both spouses must qualify if applying jointly. This status unlocks: 5% down payments, priority processing, reserved loan funds, additional crop insurance subsidies, and eligibility for the Down Payment Program.
What loans are available specifically for beginning farmers?
FSA: Direct Ownership (5% down), Direct Operating ($600K max), Microloans ($50K simplified), Down Payment Program (5% down + FSA 45% + lender 50%). Farm Credit: reduced-rate programs (0.25–0.50% below standard). States: tax credits, linked deposits, and grants. These programs stack — a beginning farmer can access federal, state, and cooperative benefits simultaneously.
Do I need farming experience to get a farm loan?
FSA requires either farm experience OR completion of borrower training (8–16 hours through Extension). Farm Credit evaluates each applicant individually. Online lenders only evaluate revenue and credit score — no farm experience needed. The borrower training option makes FSA accessible even to first-generation farmers with no agricultural background.
What is the FSA Down Payment Program?
This lets beginning farmers buy land with only 5% down. FSA provides up to 45% of purchase price (max $300,150) at a fixed rate for 20 years. A participating commercial lender covers the remaining 50%. This three-way structure makes land accessible to farmers with limited savings. The property must be your primary agricultural operation.
Are there grants for beginning farmers?
Direct grants are limited: USDA Value-Added Producer Grants (up to $250,000), EQIP cost-share for conservation practices, and state-specific programs. Most beginning farmer support comes as subsidized loans, tax credits, and premium discounts — not outright grants. Nonprofit programs (Farm Aid, National Young Farmers Coalition) also provide some direct support.
How do I write a farm business plan for a loan?
Include: executive summary, operation description, market analysis, production plan, financial projections (3–5 years), marketing strategy, and management background. FSA provides template Form FSA-2037. Extension offices offer free assistance. Lenders evaluate: debt-to-asset ratio under 40%, positive cash flow by year 2–3, realistic yield and price assumptions, and risk management strategy.

State Programs & Local Resources

How do I find my local FSA Service Center?
Visit farmers.gov/service-locator and enter your zip code or county. Every agricultural county has an FSA office. Hours are typically Monday–Friday, 8:00 AM–4:30 PM. Walk-ins accepted for general questions; appointments recommended for loan discussions. You can also call the USDA hotline at 1-800-727-9540 for routing to your local office.
Do states offer their own farm loan programs?
Yes — most agricultural states offer complementary programs: beginning farmer tax credits, state agricultural finance authority participations, linked deposit programs (state-subsidized bank rates), value-added producer grants, and ag development incentives. Check your state Department of Agriculture website. These stack on top of federal FSA programs.
How does farmland price vary by state?
Prices range from under $1,000/acre (western rangeland) to $50,000+ (California vineyards). Midwest: Iowa $8,200, Illinois $9,500, Indiana $8,400 per acre average. Plains: Nebraska $5,400, Kansas $3,800. Values depend on soil productivity, water access, drainage quality, and proximity to markets and infrastructure.
What is agricultural mediation?
Free services in 37 states that help farmers and lenders resolve disputes without foreclosure. Certified mediators facilitate negotiations on payment modifications, restructuring, or alternatives. Funded by USDA and available through state Departments of Agriculture. Contact your state mediation program if you're having difficulty communicating with your lender.
Are there programs for minority and women farmers?
USDA designates Socially Disadvantaged Farmers (minorities and women) for priority FSA loan access, reduced rates, and reserved funding. The 2501 Program provides outreach and technical assistance. Many states have parallel programs. Recent legislation has expanded support including targeted debt relief and increased funding for underserved communities.
